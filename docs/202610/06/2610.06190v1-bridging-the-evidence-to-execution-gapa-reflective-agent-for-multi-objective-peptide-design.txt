Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) can reason over scientific literature and formulate complex design strategies, yet they struggle to translate these strategies into reliable biological sequence manipulations. Protein language models and related generators capture complex sequence patterns but are not inherently equipped to integrate literature evidence into multi-step reasoning and reflective decision-making. This separation creates an evidence-to-execution gap between scientific reasoning and sequence-level control. We introduce EASER (Evidence-Aware Sequence Engineering with Reflection), a reflective agent that connects scientific reasoning with sequence generation through a learned property interface. The interface consists of property-specific low-rank matrices trained offline and kept fixed during search. The agent combines these matrices to steer a specialized diffusion generator. Using retrieved biological evidence, sequence context, and prior outcomes, it proposes intervention hypotheses specifying anchors, editable positions, and control coefficients. Probe-and-Steer tests these interventions and uses predicted property responses to allocate further samples. The agent reflects on the outcomes to guide subsequent decisions. We evaluate EASER on computational antimicrobial peptide design, balancing activity, non-hemolysis, and non-toxicity. At matched decision states, explicit hypothesis formulation yields larger multi-objective gains than direct action generation. Ablations support the contributions of evidence retrieval, episodic history, reflection, and Probe-and-Steer. Across six independent runs, EASER achieves the highest mean hypervolume and lowest mean IGD+ among the evaluated search policies and generators on screened candidates. The AMP experiments illustrate how an executable property interface and iterative feedback connect scientific reasoning with specialized sequence generation. 

# 1 Introduction 

Scientific literature offers interventional principles for biological sequence design, specifying changes and the conditions under which they may help. Residue substitutions, for example, can alter both antibacterial activity and hemolysis [Chen et al., 2007]. Applying this knowledge requires an interface for making executable changes to a particular sequence. Large language models (LLMs) can reason over these principles, but textual plans do not reliably produce sequences with the intended properties [Ge et al., 2026]. Sequence generators learn complex sequence patterns [Wang et al., 2024b], yet generation alone does not interpret evidence, select inter-ventions, or reflect on outcomes. We call the difficulty of translating scientific evidence into executable sequence interventions the evidence-to-execution gap. Antimicrobial peptide (AMP) design makes the gap concrete: activity must be balanced against hemolysis and toxicity, and hydrophobicity changes can affect antibacterial activity and hemolysis differently [Chen et al., 2007]. Applying general principles therefore requires sequence-specific decisions and evaluation of their joint consequences. Existing approaches connect language and generation through paired text–sequence learning, reward-based optimization, or tool-using agents. ProteinDT aligns text and protein representations, while ProtDAT conditions generation on protein descriptions [Liu et al., 2025, Guo et al., 2025]. Reward and preference methods specify desired outcomes [Wang et al., 2025a, Widatalla et al., 2026], while 1arXiv:2610.06190v1 [cs.AI] 5 Oct 2026 Figure 1: EASER overview. We study an interface through which an agent uses retrieved evidence and observed responses to select property-trained controls, sequence anchors, and editable positions. We introduce EASER (Evidence-Aware Sequence Engineering with Reflection), a reflective agent with composable controls over a frozen peptide diffusion generator (Figure 1). Property-specific low-rank matrices are learned offline at the output projection. The agent combines them through bounded coefficients while selecting anchors and editable positions. The matrices define the available operations; evidence guides the agent’s choice of when and where to apply them. Because controls interact, the agent treats interventions as testable hypotheses. Probe-and-Steer tests small batches and allocates further samples from their joint predicted responses. Reflection links actions to outcomes for subsequent decisions. The agent checks literature-derived expectations against computational feedback while keeping the generator and matrices fixed. We test whether interventions chosen from evidence improve AMP search using matched-state comparisons, component ablations, and analyses of coupled property responses. Across six runs of 2,560 candidates per method, EASER achieves the highest mean screened hypervolume and lowest mean IGD+ among the evaluated policies and generators. 

Contributions. We provide (1) an executable interface of composable low-rank property controls; (2) an evidence-grounded reflective agent that tests interventions through Probe-and-Steer; and (3) empirical validation of improved AMP search and intervention decisions, supported by component ablations and analysis of coupled responses. 

# 2 Related Work 

Generative peptide models. AMPGAN v2 and AMPGPT2 use adversarial and autoregressive generation [Van Oort et al., 2021, Clemens et al., 2026], while ProT-Diff and AMPGen use diffusion [Wang et al., 2024a, Jin et al., 2025]. AMP-Designer combines prompt tuning and reinforcement learning [Wang et al., 22025b]; HMAMP and MOFormer address multiple objectives through hypervolume optimization and Pareto ranking [Wang et al., 2025d,c]. EASER focuses on the agent’s decision process: using domain evidence and search history to select interventions and revising decisions from observed responses. 

Controllable generation. Preference optimization learns from paired outputs; DRAKES combines rewards with divergence regularization, and ProteinDPO uses experimental stability preferences [Rafailov et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2025a, Widatalla et al., 2026]. TR2-D2 trains on tree-selected trajectories, while token-aligned preference optimization learns property-conditioned preferences [Tang et al., 2025b, Zervou et al., 2025]. Classifier and classifier-free guidance steer diffusion sampling; PepTune and MP2D incorporate multi-objective tree search [Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021, Ho and Salimans, 2022, Tang et al., 2025a, Kong et al., 2026]. LoRA, model soups, and Rewarded Soups support compact updates and combinations of adapted models [Hu et al., 2022, Wortsman et al., 2022, Rame et al., 2023]. These controls express desired outcomes through rewards, labels, conditioning, or predictor-guided search. EASER connects residue- and property-level evidence to decisions about anchors, editable positions, and coefficients for property-trained matrices. 

Scientific agents. ReAct interleaves reasoning with actions, while Reflexion incorporates textual feedback into subsequent decisions [Yao et al., 2023, Shinn et al., 2023]. In scientific applications, ChemCrow and Coscientist connect language models to chemistry tools and experimental execution, and SciToolAgent uses a tool knowledge graph to support workflow selection [M. Bran et al., 2024, Boiko et al., 2023, Ding et al., 2025]. ProtAgents, OSDA Agent, and the Virtual Lab coordinate domain expertise and tools for scientific tasks [Ghafarollahi and Buehler, 2024, Hu et al., 2025, Swanson et al., 2025]. For peptide design, PepCraft coordinates generation and verification, while Pepti-Agent connects generation, prediction, and mutation through feedback [Jung et al., 2026, Chen et al., 2026]. ProtoCycle couples reflective planning with scaffold retrieval, functional-site design, and evaluation, and trains its planner through supervised fine-tuning and online reinforcement learning [Ge et al., 2026]. These systems connect planning, tools, and feedback in scientific workflows. In multi-objective sequence design, evidence can support competing modifications to the same candidate. EASER addresses this decision by testing hypotheses through learned property controls and revising subsequent interventions from their joint responses. MAC-AMP connects multi-agent assessment to generator adaptation through dynamically designed rewards and successive PPO updates [Zhou et al., 2026a]. Reward selection uses Pareto comparisons, but each PPO stage optimizes a scalar objective that need not preserve diverse activity–safety trade-offs. EASER tests evidence-informed interventions on a frozen generator and uses joint property responses to guide sampling. 

# 3 Method 

EASER searches for favorable predicted activity–safety trade-offs under a fixed generation budget. The agent uses evidence and prior outcomes to select anchors, editable positions, and property-control coefficients, then uses observed responses to guide further sampling (Figure 1). 

3.1 Low-rank output interventions 

Low-rank updates at the output projection alter amino-acid sampling preferences while the pretrained transformer supplies contextual features. Weighted combinations apply several property controls in one generation call. Our AMP-adapted Diffusion Protein Language Model (DPLM) [Wang et al., 2024b] undergoes sequential LoRA adaptation on short peptides and an AMP-oriented corpus (Appendix B.2). 3.2 Evidence-informed intervention hypotheses 

Sequence-conditioned retrieval supplies organism, residue, and motif evidence to three property specialists (Figure 2). The specialists aggregate supporting and adverse evidence deterministically; LLM calls generate joint proposals, allocate samples, and reflect on outcomes. The LLM combines candidate sequences, predicted properties, physicochemical summaries, specialist recommendations, and previous outcomes to formulate intervention hypotheses. Each hypothesis specifies anchor sequences P , editable positions M , control coefficients c, and shared strength η, together with expected effects and risks. For example, modifying a hydrophobic region may reduce hemolysis while risking a loss of activity. An empty anchor set specifies controlled de novo sampling. Appendix C.1 describes the evidence resource. Probe-and-Steer evaluates four hypotheses before committing further samples to them. Probe gener-ates 16 candidates per hypothesis; their joint predicted responses guide the allocation of 160 Steer samples, with each intervention held fixed. Another 32 unsteered de novo samples introduce new candidates, bringing the round total to 256. The archive supplies anchors for subsequent refinement. 4After Steer sampling, the agent summarizes which actions succeeded or failed and what to try next. These summaries and numerical history inform the next proposal. Appendix C.1 describes this context and gives the procedure in Algorithm 1; Appendix F traces its use in a recorded run. 

3.3 Learning the property directions 

We learn each control offline from preferred and dispreferred peptide pairs matched by coarse physico-chemical descriptors. Activity preferences use MIC, low-hemolysis preferences use HC50, and toxicity preferences use curated labels. A reference-relative masked preference objective trains the low-rank factors using shared diffusion corruption, replay, and norm regularization. We select checkpoints by validation performance and calibrate their norms before search. Appendix B.2 details the objective, preference pairs, and training settings. 

# 4 Experimental Setup 

Task and comparisons. We compared EASER with five generators (AMPGAN, AMPGPT2, AMPGen, AMPDesigner, and AMP-adapted DPLM) on predicted AMP activity, non-hemolysis, and non-toxicity. To examine intervention selection, we compared EASER with fixed coefficients, random actions, LinUCB, and NSGA-II using the same frozen generator and matrices. LinUCB and NSGA-II selected anchors, masks, coefficients, and strength within the shared action space. Component ablations and matched-information comparisons examined the roles of evidence and feedback; random-matrix controls tested the learned interventions. 

Evaluation protocol. Main comparisons used six runs of 2,560 candidates per method, with lengths of 15–35 residues and ten rounds of 256 candidates. Component ablations and full-search matrix replacement used three matched seeds. Internal predictors supplied feedback; external predictors and the evaluation front were withheld from decisions. Common normalization and physicochemical penalties applied to all methods, with screening requiring all three adjusted utilities above 0.5 (Appendices A.1 and A.2). 

Metrics. Hypervolume (HV) and modified inverted generational distance (IGD+) [Ishibuchi et al., 2015] measured joint performance on screened candidates using normalized scores before penalty. HV used reference (0 .5, 0.5, 0.5) ; IGD+ used a fixed empirical front (Appendix A.2). Property-specific top-ten means used separate selected sets. We report equal-weight run means and sample SD; sensitivity analyses reuse the same candidates.

5.3 Learned controls produce coupled property responses 

Property-specific control. Each learned matrix increased its target score under positive control in de novo sampling. At c = 1 , mean gains were 0.03188 for activity, 0.03170 for non-hemolysis, and 0.06996 for non-toxicity, exceeding all three spectrum-matched random controls. Target means increased monotonically across the five nonnegative coefficients. These means include all 500 draws per condition before screening, with matched lengths and sampling schedules (Appendices D.2 and D.3). 

Combining controls expands the observed trade-offs. Joint settings reached mean property combi-nations unavailable in single-matrix scans. At the common 62,500-draw grid budget, learned controls produced pooled screened HV of 0.04957 versus 0.03516–0.03844 for random sets. Across 124 nonzero combinations, more learned settings improved each adjusted mean by over 0.01 (Appendix D.3). The coefficient settings that maximized each KDE-adjusted property mean differed across objectives. Ap-pendix D.1 examines the underlying residue preferences and contextual variation. 

Control orientation matters within adaptive search. Replacing all three learned matrices with spectrum- and norm-matched random directions reduced mean search HV by 21.4% and increased screened yield (Table 6). The agent selected actions using feedback from each fixed random matrix set (Appendix C.5).

# 6 Discussion and Conclusion 

EASER connects evidence-grounded decisions to sequence generation through learned property controls. Learned matrices produced larger property responses than spectrum-matched random controls, while hypothesis formulation improved intervention outcomes under matched information. EASER learns reusable sequence operations offline, then uses evidence and feedback to choose interventions during search. The control grid revealed property-dependent coefficient optima and both favorable and adverse trade-offs. Probe-and-Steer observes these joint responses before allocating further samples. Reflection links the responses to executed actions so later proposals can use this experience.

Domain specialists. Three specialists deterministically synthesize supporting, adverse, and mixed evidence into property-specific recommendations, confidence, and risks; conflicts reduce confidence. The LLM controller makes the joint design decision. 

Main controller. The deepseek-v4-flash controller proposes four interventions per round from property evidence, visible sequences, and prior outcomes. Probe feedback guides further sampling among them.

Execution and budgets. Algorithm 1 allocates 32/64/160 samples per round. The frozen 650M backbone uses 63,024 learned factor parameters stored as 126,720 matrix entries, without online gradients. Candidates failing final screening remain eligible parents. 

C.2 Numerical controllers 

Numerical controllers share EASER’s generator, matrices, length range, and six run seeds. Runs comprise ten rounds of 256 candidates: 32 de novo, 64 Probe, and 160 Steer per round. 

Shared action space. Both baselines select from all candidates in the same design context. Random-key encoding supports any parent subset, including none for de novo sampling, and any nonempty position subset up to half each parent’s length. Coefficients span [−1, 1] 3; strength spans [0 , 0.5] . The prior favors small parent sets with nonzero support for every legal subset. 

NSGA-II. Online NSGA-II uses nondominated sorting and crowding [Deb et al., 2002]. Round one probes four random actions; later rounds probe two survivors, remapped to the current context and reevaluated, plus two offspring. Simulated binary crossover uses index 15; polynomial mutation uses index 20 and probability 1/d for encoding dimension d. Twenty percent of offspring proposals are fresh random actions; each round selects two survivors. 

LinUCB. The contextual-bandit rule of Li et al. [2010] selects interventions. Each round pools 256 fresh actions with retained and mutated survivors, without generating peptides. Features summarize effective coefficients, parent properties and composition, masked residues, position statistics, and context. An identity ridge prior and exploration multiplier 1 rank actions by reward plus uncertainty; four distinct actions are probed. Reward is 0.4a + 0 .3h + 0 .3t.Distinct sequence-level actions can share the same feature vector. 

Feedback and allocation. Both algorithms batch-center KDE-penalized internal activity, non-hemolysis, and non-toxicity objectives (a, h, t ), without KL penalties or external evaluation during search. Steer allocation follows nondomination ranks in eight-sample quanta, with at least eight and at most 120 per proposal, conserving the 160-sample budget. 

C.3 Matched-information comparison 

The comparison in Table 4 uses the ten pre-Probe states from each of three campaigns. Both policies receive identical sequences, internal scores, specialist claims, and history, augmented with residue counts, indexed sequences, and descriptors for this comparison. Explicit formulation states four hypotheses with supporting observations and risks; direct generation returns four actions. Both use DeepSeek Flash (API identifier deepseek-flash ) at temperature zero with the API thinking option disabled. The generator and matrices are fixed, and sampling seeds are paired. Each action produces 16 candidates, giving 64 candidates per state and 1,920 per policy. The hypotheses cited 360 observations, all consistent with the supplied values. After bounded correction, one proposal had a mismatch between its mask and rationale. Full masking specifies de novo sampling. We measure each state’s HV gain over all pre-Probe candidates, including the current round’s initial de novo batch. Both policies use equal sampling allocations, the common external screen, and the unpenalized HV reference (0 .5, 0.5, 0.5) .

The experiments evaluated predicted AMP activity–safety trade-offs and CPP classification scores. AMP design used the complete agent; CPP transfer used a fixed matrix-control sweep.
