Abstract—Instruction tuning has become a common paradigm
for applying large language models (LLMs) to biomedical named
entity recognition (BioNER). However, existing instruction-tuning
approaches still face two key challenges. First, conventional
natural-language instructions typically serialize BioNER an-
notations as flat textual outputs, providing limited structural
constraints for typed entity extraction. Second, high-quality
biomedical annotations are limited, and learning from a single
serialized output form may restrict structural diversity and re-
duce model robustness. Although external biomedical knowledge
can be introduced to alleviate data scarcity, it often requires
costly resource construction. To address these challenges, we
propose MITE, a Multiple Programming Languages Instruction
Tuning and Ensemble method for BioNER. MITE reformulates
BioNER as a structure-to-structure generation task by represent-
ing both instructions and entity outputs in code-formatted rep-
resentations. Specifically, each training instance is transformed
into multiple programming-language formats, including Python,
C++, and Java, while preserving the same underlying entity
semantics. These language-specific representations provide struc-
turally diverse supervision without requiring external biomedical
knowledge or additional annotations. During inference, MITE
aggregates predictions from different code formats through an
entity-level voting strategy, reducing language-specific prediction
variance and improving robustness. Experiments on six widely
used BioNER datasets demonstrate that MITE consistently out-
performs representative BERT-based and LLM-based baselines
and exhibits strong cross-dataset generalization. Ablation and pa-
rameter analyses further verify the effectiveness and robustness
of the proposed components.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have
opened up new opportunities for BioNER by enabling mod-
els to follow task-specific instructions and generate flexible
textual outputs. To adapt general-purpose LLMs to biomed-
ical entity extraction, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) has been
widely explored, where BioNER instances are converted into
instruction-following formats and used to teach models how
to extract entities from biomedical text [4], [5]. Under this
paradigm, natural language instructions are commonly used
to describe the extraction task, specify the target entity types,
and guide the model to produce structured predictions. To

further improve extraction performance, several recent studies
incorporate external biomedical knowledge or domain-specific
resources, allowing models to better capture specialized termi-
nology and biomedical semantics [6], [7].
Despite notable progress in BioNER, existing studies still
suffer from two main limitations. First, BioNER is inherently
a span-type structure prediction task, requiring models to
identify entity boundaries and assign semantic types under a
predefined schema. However, current instruction-tuning meth-
ods usually formulate entity extraction as natural-language-
to-text generation, where entity annotations are serialized into
flat textual outputs. Such a formulation weakens the alignment
between typed entity structures and the code-like structured
patterns learned by LLMs during pretraining, limiting their
ability to exploit explicit structural regularities for BioNER
prediction, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). Second, high-quality
biomedical annotations are limited, and existing instruction-
tuning methods usually learn BioNER from a single serialized
output form. This restricts the structural diversity of training
signals and may make models sensitive to specific output
formats rather than robust entity semantics. Although external
biomedical knowledge can be introduced to alleviate data
scarcity, such resources are often costly to construct, limited
in coverage, and difficult to maintain.
To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose
MITE, a Multiple Programming Languages Instruction Tun-
ing and Ensemble method for BioNER. MITE reformu-
lates BioNER from natural-language-to-text generation into
a structure-to-structure generation task, where both task in-
structions and entity outputs are represented in code-formatted
structures. Entity mentions and their semantic types are ex-
pressed as explicit code-style objects, providing a constrained
output representation that better aligns span-type BioNER
prediction with the code-like structured patterns learned by
LLMs during pretraining, as illustrated in Fig. 1(b). To further
improve robustness, MITE transforms each BioNER instance
into multiple programming-language representations, includ-
ing Python, C++, and Java. These representations introduce
structural diversity through different syntax forms, object
construction patterns, and list operations, without requiring ex-
ternal biomedical knowledge or additional annotations. During
inference, MITE aggregates predictions from different code
formats through entity-level voting, reducing language-specific
prediction variance and producing more stable BioNER out-
puts.
In summary, the contributions of this paper are as follows:
• We reformulate BioNER as a structure-to-structure code
generation task, which represents span-type entity pre-
dictions as explicit code-style objects and better aligns
BioNER with the structured patterns learned by LLMs
during pretraining.
• We transform original BioNER samples into multiple
programming-language representations, constructing a
structurally diverse training corpus without relying on
external biomedical knowledge or additional annotations.
• We introduce a multi-language entity-level ensemble
strategy that aggregates predictions from different code
representations to improve the stability and reliability of
BioNER outputs.
• Extensive experiments on six BioNER benchmarks
demonstrate that MITE consistently outperforms rep-
resentative baselines and exhibits strong cross-dataset
generalization.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Biomedical Named Entity Recognition
BioNER has progressed from feature-based systems to
neural architectures and domain-specific pretrained language
models. Early systems relied on handcrafted rules, biomedical
dictionaries, and manually designed features, requiring sub-
stantial expert knowledge and generalizing poorly to unseen
terminology [8]. Neural models based on CNNs and BiL-
STMs subsequently reduced manual feature engineering by
learning contextual and character-level representations from
annotated corpora [9]. More recently, pretrained models such
as BioBERT, BlueBERT, ClinicalBERT, and PubMedBERT
have achieved strong performance by learning domain-specific
representations from large-scale biomedical corpora [10], [11].
Despite these advances, conventional BioNER methods pre-
dominantly follow the sequence-labeling paradigm and require
dataset-specific adaptation. This limits their transferability
across biomedical corpora with different entity types, anno-
tation guidelines, and label granularities. Multi-task learning
methods improve cross-dataset transfer by jointly training on
multiple corpora [12], [13], while AIONER further maps
heterogeneous annotations into a unified label space [14].
However, directly combining multiple datasets may introduce
annotation inconsistencies because the same biomedical men-
tion can be assigned different labels or omitted across cor-
pora. Achieving cross-dataset generalization while preserving
dataset-specific label semantics therefore remains challenging.
B. LLM-Based Information Extraction
LLMs have promoted a shift from task-specific BioNER ar-
chitectures toward generative and instruction-following infor-
mation extraction. Existing studies employ zero-shot prompt-
ing [15], [16], few-shot demonstrations [17], and super-
vised instruction
tuning [18], [19]
to guide
LLMs in
extracting structured information. In the biomedical do-
main, BioNER-LLaMA formulates entity recognition as an
instruction-following generation task [4], NERLlama3.1 per-
forms parameter-efficient multi-dataset instruction tuning [5],
and VANER incorporates external biomedical knowledge into
natural-language instructions [6]. These approaches demon-
strate the potential of LLMs for generative BioNER across
multiple datasets.
However, natural-language instructions and free-form de-
coding provide limited explicit control over the structured
outputs required by BioNER, including entity boundaries,
semantic types, and output formats [20]. Recent code-oriented
methods address this issue by expressing extraction schemas

III. METHODS
We propose MITE, a Multiple Programming Languages
Instruction Tuning and Ensemble method for BioNER. MITE
reformulates BioNER as a structure-to-structure generation
problem, where both the input instruction and the target
entity output are represented in code-like formats. For each
BioNER instance, MITE constructs language-specific code
representations in Python, C++, and Java, fine-tunes a large
language model on the resulting multi-language corpus, and
aggregates predictions from different code formats during
inference. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the framework consists
of four components: (1) task formulation, (2) multi-language
code instruction modeling, (3) multi-language supervised fine-
tuning, and (4) multi-language ensemble inference
A. Task Formulation
Given a biomedical token sequence X = (x1, x2, . . . , xn)
and a predefined entity type set T , BioNER aims to extract
a set of biomedical entities E = {(ej, tj)}m
j=1. Each entity
mention ej = (xsj, . . . , xkj) is a contiguous text span in
X, and tj ∈T denotes its semantic type. Different from
sequence-labeling methods that predict token-level tags, MITE
formulates BioNER as a generative structured prediction task,
where the model directly produces entity objects specifying
entity mentions and their semantic types.
B. Multi-Language Code Instruction Modeling
Natural-language instructions are easy to construct but of-
ten specify the output schema only implicitly, which may
lead to unstable entity formats or invalid type assignments.
To impose stronger structural constraints, MITE represents
BioNER instances as code-formatted instructions and outputs.
The key idea is to express the extraction schema through
function signatures, task descriptions, and entity construction
statements, so that the model learns to generate structured
entity objects rather than free-form textual descriptions.
1) Code-Based Instruction: For each input sentence, we
construct a function-based instruction template. The function
name indicates the BioNER task, the input argument stores


the biomedical sentence, and the docstring specifies the target
entity types. The expected output is represented as entity con-
struction statements added to an EntityList. For example,
an extracted gene mention is represented as an entity object
such as Gene("..."), rather than as an unconstrained
text span. This design makes the output schema explicit and
provides a unified interface for representing entity mentions
and semantic types.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. BioNER Datasets
In this paper, we organize the BioNER benchmarks into
source-training datasets and external transfer datasets. The
source-training set consists of three type-specific datasets
covering different biomedical entity categories: BC2GM [23]
for gene recognition, BC5CDR-Chemical [24] for chemical
recognition, and the NCBI Disease Corpus [25] for disease
recognition. The three source datasets are combined for super-
vised instruction tuning, yielding a single MITE model shared
across gene, chemical, and disease recognition tasks.
To evaluate the generalization capability of the proposed
method across different datasets within the same entity
categories, we introduce three additional publicly available
BioNER datasets as external validation benchmarks: NLM-
Gene [26] for gene recognition, NLM-Chem-BC7 [27] for
chemical recognition, and BC5CDR-Disease [24] for disease
recognition. The trained MITE model is directly used for infer-
ence on these external transfer datasets. For BC5CDR-Disease,
only the chemical annotations from BC5CDR-Chemical are
used during source training, while disease annotations are
not used, thereby avoiding target-label leakage in the disease
transfer setting. The detailed statistics and dataset splits for all
datasets are summarized in Table I.

B. Baselines
To evaluate the effectiveness of MITE, we compare it
against two categories of baseline approaches: BERT-based
methods and LLM-based methods.
1) BERT-based Methods:
• BioBERT [10] extends the original BERT architecture
by further adapting it to the biomedical domain through
large-scale pretraining on PubMed abstracts and PMC
full-text articles.
• PubMedBERT [11] adopts a domain-centric pretraining
strategy by training the model entirely from scratch on
PubMed data, including a vocabulary constructed exclu-
sively from biomedical texts.
• AIONER [14] integrates annotation labels from multiple
datasets to construct a unified sequence labeling task,
enhancing multi-task learning performance.
2) LLM-based Methods:
• InstructUIE [18] leverages Flan-T5 to unify a range of
information extraction tasks under a text-to-text formula-
tion.
• UniversalNER [19] distills ChatGPT into LLaMA-2 for
NER as a natural language generation task across multiple
domains via instruction tuning.
• PMC-LLaMA: [28] undergoes domain-specific pretrain-
ing on biomedical corpora, followed by supervised fine-
tuning for medical QA and conversational tasks, and is
finally specialized for BioNER.
• BioNER-LLaMA [4] transforms biomedical NER into
a generation task using instruction tuning on LLaMA-2
across multiple datasets.
• VANER [6] is a knowledge-enhanced, instruction-tuned
generative framework based on LLaMA-2 that incorpo-
rates UMLS entity knowledge and multi-task training.
• NERLlama3.1

C. Evaluation Metrics
Following the standard practice in BioNER literature [29],
model performance is primarily assessed using the Micro-F1
score. A prediction is regarded as correct only when both the
entity boundaries and the assigned label exactly coincide with
the standard annotations.
D. Experiment Settings
In our experiments, we fine-tune several pretrained large
language models, including LLaMA2-7B [30], CodeLLaMA-
7B [31], Qwen2.5-7B [32], and Qwen2.5-coder-7B [33]. To
achieve efficient training with limited resources, we adopt the
QLoRA approach, setting the rank to 32, alpha to 64, and
applying a dropout rate of 0.05. Each model undergoes 15
epochs of training using the AdamW optimizer, with a batch
size of 8 and a maximum sequence length of 2048 tokens.
We initialize the learning rate at 6e-5 and adjust it using a
cosine scheduler with a warm-up proportion of 0.05. All the
experiments take place on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU.
V. RESULTS AND ANALYSIS
A. Main Results
Table II reports the main experimental results on three
widely used BioNER benchmarks. Overall, MITE consistently
achieves the best performance across all evaluated datasets,
establishing a new state of the art among both BERT-based and
LLM-based approaches. Compared with representative BERT-
based methods, which are pretrained from scratch on PubMed
biomedical corpora, MITE demonstrates superior performance.
We attribute this superiority to the strong language understand-
ing and generalization capabilities inherent in its underlying
large language model, which is pretrained on a trillion-token
scale. In comparison with the LLM-based methods such as
UniversalNER, BioNER-LLaMA, and NERLlama3.1, MITE
also achieves consistent improvements. These methods for-
mulate BioNER using natural language prompts and treat the
task as free-form text generation, while we adopt a code-
based instruction paradigm that explicitly constrains the output
structure. This formulation enables the model to leverage the
structured output capability and context-aware abbreviation
understanding ability acquired during pretraining on large-
scale code corpora, leading to more stable entity boundary
detection and type prediction. In particular, compared with
VANER, which enhances BioNER performance by incorporat-
ing external biomedical knowledge, MITE achieves superior
results without relying on any external resources. Instead, the
improvements are attributed to the proposed multiple program-
ming languages data augmentation strategy during training
and the multiple programming languages ensemble inference
strategy during testing, which together enhance robustness and
prediction stability.

C. Ablation Study
To analyze the contribution of each programming-language
representation, we remove one language at a time from the
multi-language training corpus while keeping all other settings
unchanged. As shown in Table IV, removing Python, C++, and
Java decreases the average Micro-F1 by 0.60, 0.46, and 0.53
points, respectively. The consistent performance drops indicate
that different programming languages provide complementary
structural information. Since the three drops are relatively
close, the gains of MITE mainly come from jointly using
multiple language representations rather than relying on a
single dominant language.
To further distinguish the effect of structural diversity from
that of data scale, we conduct controlled experiments under
the settings shown in Table V. Single-language settings, in-
cluding Python1×, C++1×, and Java1×, use one programming-
language representation. Their 3× variants enlarge the training
corpus by changing output order and docstring descriptions
while preserving the same programming-language structure.
The results show that simply increasing the single-language
corpus brings only limited gains. Python3× improves over
Python1× by 0.08 points, while C++3× and Java3× improve by

D. The Effect of LLM Backbone
BioNER requires accurate biomedical semantic understand-
ing, while MITE further introduces code-style structured
generation. Therefore, we investigate whether code-oriented
LLMs are more suitable than base LLMs under the pro-
posed instruction-tuning framework. Specifically, LLaMA2-
7B and Qwen2.5-7B are categorized as base models, while
CodeLLaMA-7B and Qwen2.5-Coder-7B are categorized as
code-oriented models.
As shown in Fig. 3, the base models consistently outperform
the code-oriented models across all three datasets, indicating
that code-oriented pretraining does not necessarily bring ad-
vantages for BioNER under code-style instruction tuning. This
trend can be attributed to the difference in pretraining data
composition. Base models are pretrained on broader natural-
language corpora and can better capture biomedical semantics
while retaining basic structural generation ability. In contrast,
code-oriented models are further specialized on source code
and technical documentation, which improves programming-
syntax modeling but may reduce their exposure to the rich
natural-language contexts required for BioNER.

E. The Effect of Ensemble Strategies
To examine the impact of inference-time aggregation,
we compare single-language prediction with a voting-based
ensemble strategy. In single-language inference, the well-
trained MITE model predicts entities using one programming-
language representation, including Python, C++, or Java. In
the voting setting, entity predictions supported by multiple
language-specific outputs are retained as the final results.
As shown in Table VI, different programming-language rep-
resentations achieve comparable but not identical performance,
indicating that the specific code format can still influence
BioNER prediction. For LLaMA2, Python obtains the best
single-language score of 89.05, followed by Java and C++
with 88.30 and 87.40, respectively. For CodeLLaMA, the
three representations produce more stable results, with scores
of 88.78, 88.60, and 88.64. The voting strategy consistently
outperforms all single-language settings, improving the best
single-language score from 89.05 to 90.14 for LLaMA2 and
from 88.78 to 89.16 for CodeLLaMA. These results show
that different programming-language representations provide
complementary predictions, and entity-level voting can reduce


language-specific prediction bias and improve the robustness
of BioNER inference.
VI. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose MITE, a Multiple Program-
ming Languages Instruction Tuning and Ensemble method
for BioNER. MITE reformulates BioNER as a structure-to-
structure generation task by representing both task instruc-
tions and entity outputs in code-formatted structures, provid-
ing stronger structural constraints for typed entity extraction
and better aligning BioNER prediction with the structured
patterns learned by LLMs during pretraining. To improve
robustness without relying on external biomedical knowledge
or additional annotations, MITE transforms each BioNER
instance into multiple programming-language representations
and aggregates their predictions through entity-level voting.
Experiments on six BioNER datasets demonstrate that MITE
consistently outperforms representative baselines, achieves
strong cross-dataset generalization, and remains effective un-
der ablation and parameter analyses.
